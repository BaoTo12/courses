## Narrative Bridge: When Success Bankrupts the Platform

In [Lecture 03.07](./03.07-debug-resultpath-state-obliteration.md), we diagnosed and resolved data corruption in our JSONPath pipelines. Now, we examine an attack vector that doesn't breach data or crash servers, but instead targets two critical enterprise vulnerabilities:
1. **Concurrency Starvation**: Hitting hard AWS service quotas under traffic spikes.
2. **Economic Denial of Service (Pricing Collapse)**: Designing workflows with billing models that turn high traffic into catastrophic financial debt.

Let's stress-test our state machine architecture under high throughput and prove why workflow engine selection is a make-or-break architectural decision.

---

## 1. Attack Scenario 1: Concurrency Starvation at 5,000 TPS

CloudOrder launches a flash sale. Upstream SQS workers immediately attempt to launch 5,000 workflow executions per second into our Standard Workflow:

```text
Incoming Ingress Load: 5,000 orders/sec
Standard Workflow Quota: 2,000 start executions/sec (burst up to 4,000)
Outcome: AWS rejects all starts above 4,000 with ExecutionLimitExceeded!
```

### The SQS Poller Crash:
1. Ingress spikes to 5,000 orders/sec.
2. Step Functions throttles `sfnClient.startExecution()`.
3. Java worker throws `ExecutionLimitExceededException`.
4. Messages return to SQS, exacerbating the queue backlog.
5. Within 5 minutes, CloudWatch alarms trigger `ExecutionsThrottled > 10,000`.

---

## 2. Attack Scenario 2: The $129,000 Flash Sale Bill Shock

Even if AWS Support increased your Standard Workflow quota to accommodate 10,000 TPS, you would walk straight into a **financial death trap**.

### The Math of Standard Workflow Pricing:
* Standard Workflows bill at **$0.025 per 1,000 state transitions**.
* Our `CloudOrderCheckoutSaga` has **6 state transitions** per run:
  `ReserveInventory` $\to$ `TaskStarted` $\to$ `ProcessPayment` $\to$ `TaskStarted` $\to$ `Complete` $\to$ `Succeeded`.

Now calculate the cost of a 24-hour flash sale sustaining 10,000 orders per second:
$$\text{Total Orders in 24 Hours} = 10,000 \times 86,400\text{ seconds} = 864,000,000\text{ orders}$$
$$\text{Total State Transitions} = 864,000,000 \times 6 = 5,184,000,000\text{ transitions}$$
$$\text{Standard Workflow Bill} = \frac{5,184,000,000}{1,000} \times \$0.025 = \mathbf{\$129,600.00 \text{ in 24 hours!}}$$

Your flash sale just incurred a **$129,600 bill** in orchestration charges alone!

---

## 3. The Architectural Defense: Switching to Express Workflows

To survive high-throughput workloads without financial ruin or concurrency limits, you must convert the state machine to an **Express Workflow**.

### Converting in SAM:
```yaml
# In template.yaml:
OrderCheckoutStateMachine:
  Type: AWS::Serverless::StateMachine
  Properties:
    Name: CloudOrderCheckoutExpressSaga
    Type: EXPRESS # ⚡ Changed from STANDARD to EXPRESS
    Role: !GetAtt OrderCheckoutStateMachineRole.Arn
```

---

## 4. The Express Workflow Economics Proof

Express Workflows do **not** bill per state transition. Instead, they bill like AWS Lambda:
1. **Requests**: $1.00 per 1,000,000 executions.
2. **Duration & Memory**: $0.00001667 per GB-second (metered in 100ms increments).

### Recalculating the 24-Hour Flash Sale (864M Executions):
* Average Execution Duration: 150ms $\to$ billed as 200ms.
* Memory Allocated: 64 MB (0.0625 GB).

$$\text{Request Cost} = \frac{864,000,000}{1,000,000} \times \$1.00 = \$864.00$$
$$\text{Compute GB-Seconds} = 864,000,000 \times 0.2\text{s} \times 0.0625\text{GB} = 10,800,000\text{ GB-s}$$
$$\text{Compute Cost} = 10,800,000 \times \$0.00001667 = \$180.04$$
$$\mathbf{\text{Total Express Workflow Bill}} = \$864.00 + \$180.04 = \mathbf{\$1,044.04}$$

### The Verdict:
| Engine | 24-Hour Flash Sale Bill | Max Start Rate |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| **Standard Workflows** | **$129,600.00** | 2,000 / sec (Throttled!) |
| **Express Workflows** | **$1,044.04** | **100,000+ / sec (Passed!)** |
| **Cost Savings** | **99.2% Reduction** | **50x Throughput** |
